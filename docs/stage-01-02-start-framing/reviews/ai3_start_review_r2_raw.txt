## 1. Strongest reason the reconstructed decision may be wrong

**The decision may be mischaracterized as a "recommendation" decision when it is actually a decision under a hard attention/throughput constraint that changes the decision object itself.**

The stakeholder's T018 correction ("My team can realistically work up about 25 price-change recommendations per four-week cycle — that's what we can get through with the GM") was treated in T019–T021 as an added constraint on a per-product classification decision. But the stakeholder's wording suggests the real decision may be a *selection and sequencing* decision: *which ~25 (or fewer) products to work up first*, not merely *which products qualify for a raise/cut/hold label*. The T021 statement resolves this by saying "limiting actual price-change recommendations to approximately 25" and "prioritized by importance," but it does not establish:

- Whether Morgan's decision is (a) classify every eligible product into raise/cut/hold/insufficient-evidence and then rank the changes, or (b) directly choose a slate of ~25 changes from a larger candidate pool, treating the rest as "not this cycle" rather than "hold."
- Whether the ~25 is a *hard cap on recommendations produced* or a *soft planning constraint* on what the team can physically prepare. T018 says "what we can get through with the GM" — that is a throughput constraint on the review meeting, not necessarily a constraint on the analyst's output. If Morgan can bring 25 changes but wants to know about 40, the decision is different from one where only 25 may be recommended at all.

The difference matters because it changes the outcome variable: if the decision is "which 25 to work up," then the objective may be to maximize expected revenue *subject to the capacity constraint across the portfolio*, not per-product. The T021 statement preserves per-product framing and bolts on a cap, which may underrepresent the portfolio-level prioritization the stakeholder actually described.

**Evidence from verbatim dialogue:** T018 — "If more products than that qualify, I need to know which ones matter most so we take the strongest first." T020 — "The 25 is about actual price changes, raises or cuts, because those are what my team has to work up and walk through with the GM." T021 — analyst converts this into "limiting actual price-change recommendations to approximately 25 per four-week review cycle" and "prioritized by importance." The analyst's rendering is plausible but not the only reading; "strongest first" could imply a ranked list longer than 25, with the top 25 worked up and the rest deferred, not suppressed.

**Why this is the strongest challenge:** It goes to the decision object itself. Every other issue below is secondary to whether the decision is per-product classification, portfolio selection under capacity, or both.

## 4. Any conflict between stated outcome and proposed action

**A. Outcome = expected product revenue over 28 days; action = raise / targeted cut / hold.**
The outcome is defined at the product level, but the capacity constraint (T018) introduces a portfolio-level consideration: if more than 25 products qualify, the team must choose which ones to work up. The stated outcome (maximize expected product revenue) is per-product, but the proposed action (prioritize strongest first) is portfolio-level. There is no stated portfolio-level outcome (e.g., maximize total expected revenue across the 25 selected changes, or maximize expected revenue subject to capacity). This is a conflict between the per-product objective and the portfolio-level selection problem. It is not necessarily fatal — one could maximize expected revenue per product and then rank by expected revenue lift — but the decision statement does not resolve whether the ranking key is expected revenue lift, expected revenue level, probability of success, or something else. T018 says "which ones matter most," which is vague. The analyst's T021 says "prioritized by importance," which is equally vague.

**B. Guardrail = reject unit declines > ~10%; action = targeted price cut.**
A targeted price cut is presumably intended to increase units. If a cut is expected to *decrease* units (which would be unusual but possible if the price cut signals lower quality or if the product is Veblen-like), the guardrail would reject it. More importantly, the guardrail is asymmetric: it only constrains unit *declines*. A raise that increases units (again unusual but possible) would not be constrained. The stated outcome (maximize revenue) and the guardrail (avoid >10% unit decline) are not in direct conflict, but the guardrail is a one-sided constraint that may not capture all customer-impact concerns. The stakeholder said "without doing something dumb to the customer" (T004) and then operationalized it as volume (T006). The proposed action set does not include a customer-impact dimension beyond unit decline. This is a potential conflict if "doing something dumb to the customer" includes things other than volume loss (e.g., perceived unfairness, competitive response, long-term brand damage). The stakeholder explicitly chose volume as the guardrail, so this is resolved for now, but it is worth noting that the guardrail is a proxy for a broader concern.

**C. Outcome = expected product revenue; information = no cost/margin.**
The stakeholder acknowledges margin is unavailable and accepts revenue (T004: "I'd love to judge it on margin, but we don't have cost in this data, so revenue is the number I can actually defend"). This is a stated conflict between the ideal outcome (margin) and the feasible outcome (revenue). The proposed action uses revenue, which is consistent with the constraint. No conflict between stated outcome and proposed action, but the conflict between ideal and feasible is worth recording as a limitation.

**D. Timing: decision at end of history; outcome window = 28 days after decision.**
The decision point is the end of the most recent history (T012). The outcome window is the following 28 days. The analysis must use only information available at the decision point (T014). This is internally consistent. However, the stakeholder also says "I need a sense of what to expect over the next four weeks at the current price versus at the price I'd be recommending" (T014). This is a forward-looking expectation, which is consistent with using historical data to predict. No conflict.

**E. Capacity = ~25 actual price changes; outcome = maximize expected revenue.**
If the team can only work up 25 changes, and more than 25 products qualify, the decision is not "which products should be changed" but "which ~25 products should be changed first." The stated outcome (maximize expected product revenue) is silent on whether to maximize total expected revenue across the selected set or to maximize per-product expected revenue and then rank. This is a conflict between the stated outcome and the proposed action (prioritize by importance) because "importance" is not defined in terms of the stated outcome. The analyst should have asked: "When more than 25 products qualify, what does 'matter most' mean — highest expected revenue lift, highest expected revenue, highest confidence, or something else?" This is a missing link between outcome and action.

## 7. Whether the analysis could actually change the decision

**Yes, but with caveats.**

The decision is a per-product recommendation (raise / targeted cut / hold) at a specific store, for eligible products, with a capacity constraint. The analysis could change the decision in several ways:

- **Evidence-based classification:** The analysis could identify products where the historical price-sales relationship suggests a raise or cut would increase expected revenue without violating the unit-decline guardrail. This would change the recommendation from "hold" to "raise" or "cut."
- **Evidence-based hold:** The analysis could identify products where the evidence is insufficient, leading to "hold — not enough evidence." This is a legitimate outcome (T016) and would change the recommendation from a forced call to an explicit hold.
- **Prioritization:** If more than 25 products qualify, the analysis could rank them by expected revenue impact, changing which products are worked up first. This is a portfolio-level change.
- **Eligibility:** The analysis could apply the eligibility rule (several price changes, steady sales) to determine which products enter the decision set. This is a scope change.

However, the analysis is limited by:

- **No cost/margin data:** Revenue is a proxy for profit. A revenue-maximizing price change may not be profit-maximizing. The stakeholder accepts this (T004), but the analysis cannot change the decision to a margin-based one.
- **No competitor prices:** The analysis cannot account for competitive response. A price change that looks good in isolation may be undermined by competitor pricing.
- **No inventory/stockouts:** The analysis cannot account for supply constraints. A price cut that increases units may not be feasible if inventory is limited.
- **No randomized price tests:** The analysis relies on observational price variation, which may be confounded by unobserved factors (e.g., promotions, seasonality, store-level shocks). This limits causal inference.
- **No customer-level data:** The analysis cannot assess heterogeneous customer responses. The guardrail is a population-level unit-decline constraint, not a customer-level welfare constraint.

**Conclusion:** The analysis could change the decision within the bounds of the available data and the stated objective. It cannot change the decision to a margin-based or competitive-response-based decision. The analysis is decision-relevant but constrained. This is a "yes, within scope" answer.

## 8. Strongest alternative decision statement

**Alternative decision statement (portfolio selection under capacity, with per-product classification as an input):**

> Morgan Lee, Pricing & Revenue Manager, must decide which eligible product-store combinations at the California pilot store's everyday grocery and household aisles to recommend for a shelf-price raise or targeted price cut in the upcoming four-week pricing cycle, and which to leave unchanged or classify as "hold — not enough evidence," in order to maximize expected total product revenue across the recommended set over the following 28 days, subject to avoiding expected unit declines greater than approximately 10% for any recommended product, using only information available at the end of the current history period, and subject to the team's capacity to prepare and review approximately 25 actual price-change recommendations with the store general manager. If more than 25 products qualify for a price change, the recommendations must be ranked by expected revenue impact (or another stakeholder-confirmed ranking key) and the top 25 worked up first; if fewer than 25 qualify, no additional recommendations should be forced. Products classified as "hold" or "hold — not enough evidence" do not consume capacity but must be listed as reviewed.

**Why this is stronger:**
- It makes explicit that the decision is a *selection* problem when more than 25 products qualify, not just a per-product classification problem.
- It clarifies that the ~25 is a capacity constraint on preparation and review, not necessarily a hard cap on the number of recommendations produced.
- It introduces "expected total product revenue across the recommended set" as the portfolio-level objective, which is consistent with the per-product revenue objective but makes the portfolio-level trade-off explicit.
- It leaves the ranking key open for stakeholder confirmation (expected revenue impact vs. another key), rather than assuming "importance" is self-evident.
- It preserves the per-product classification (raise / cut / hold / hold — not enough evidence) as an input to the selection decision.

**Alternative decision statement (simpler, if the stakeholder confirms that the ~25 is a hard cap and per-product classification is the primary decision):**

> Morgan Lee, Pricing & Revenue Manager, must decide, for each eligible product-store combination at the California pilot store's everyday grocery and household aisles, whether to recommend raising the shelf price, taking a targeted price cut, or leaving the shelf price unchanged, in order to maximize expected product revenue over the following 28 days, subject to avoiding expected unit declines greater than approximately 10%, using only information available at the end of the current history period, and recommending no more than approximately 25 actual price changes per four-week cycle. When evidence is insufficient, the product should be classified as "hold — not enough evidence" rather than forcing a price change. If more than 25 products qualify for price changes, the recommendations should be ranked by expected revenue impact and the top 25 recommended; if fewer qualify, no additional recommendations should be forced. Products classified as "hold" or "hold — not enough evidence" do not consume capacity but must be listed as reviewed.

**Why this is stronger than T021:**
- It specifies that the ranking is by expected revenue impact (or another confirmed key), not vague "importance."
- It clarifies what happens to products ranked 26+: they are not recommended this cycle, but they are presumably still classified (hold or hold — not enough evidence) and listed.
- It makes the cap explicit as a recommendation limit, which is what T021 implies but does not state as clearly.
